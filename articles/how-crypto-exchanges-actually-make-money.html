The Mechanics

How crypto exchanges actually make money

Trading fees are the visible revenue line. The bigger, quieter ones are the bid-ask spread, listing fees, and what the exchange does with customer assets in the background.

A crypto exchange is, mechanically, a matching engine plus a set of adjacent businesses built around the order book. Understanding where the money actually comes from — not just the headline trading fee — explains a lot about why exchanges push certain products (staking, launchpads, leveraged trading) so hard.

Trading fees: the visible line

Most exchanges charge a "maker" fee (for orders that add liquidity to the book) and a "taker" fee (for orders that remove it), typically a small fraction of a percent per trade, often with taker fees higher than maker fees to incentivize liquidity provision. At high volume, this is the largest and most predictable revenue source for a typical exchange, and it scales directly with trading activity — which is why exchanges are financially motivated to encourage frequent trading.

The bid-ask spread

Separate from the stated fee, the gap between the best buy price and best sell price on the order book is itself a cost to traders, and on exchanges that also run market-making operations, that spread can be a direct revenue source. This cost is less visible than a stated fee percentage because it isn't itemized on a receipt — it's baked into the execution price.

Listing fees and launchpads

New token projects commonly pay exchanges to be listed — a mechanism that gives the project trading volume and visibility, and gives the exchange a fee plus, often, an allocation of the token itself. "Launchpad" programs that let users buy new tokens early typically work similarly: the exchange earns a fee or allocation for providing distribution.

What happens to idle customer assets

Assets sitting in exchange custody aren't always simply held — some exchanges lend them out, use them in market-making, or otherwise deploy them, generating a return the exchange keeps (sometimes sharing a portion via a "yield" or "earn" product). This is also the mechanism behind several well-publicized exchange collapses, where customer assets used this way weren't fully available when withdrawals surged — a mechanics point worth understanding, not a comment on any specific current platform.

Revenue streams at a glance

StreamMechanic
Trading feesSmall % per trade, maker/taker split
Bid-ask spreadGap between buy/sell price, less visible than stated fees
Listing/launchpad feesProjects pay for exchange access and distribution
Asset deploymentLending or using custodied assets for a return
Withdrawal/network feesOften marked up above the actual blockchain network fee

The actual question worth asking

Not "is this exchange's trading fee low" but "how does this exchange make money overall, and does that incentive structure align with mine as a user." An exchange earning heavily from asset deployment has a different risk profile than one earning purely from trading fees — a mechanics distinction, not a recommendation either way.
